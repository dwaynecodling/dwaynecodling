> *AI has replaced parts of my job: mostly the admin. That's freed up more of my week for the parts that need me.*

A Head of Development's week is fragmented. Calls, pull request reviews, client updates, planning, time logs, a dozen Slack threads, and somewhere in the middle of all that, the actual thinking. None of it looks like a single task you sit down and finish.

That's exactly where AI has earned its place for me. Not writing the code for the team, but taking the weight off everything around it.

## Where it earns its keep

**My day starts with a catch-up.** Instead of opening Slack, email, my call notes and our task board one by one, AI pulls together what's moved since I last looked and, more importantly, what needs me. Who's waiting on an answer. What I said I'd do in yesterday's call. It's the part of the job that used to slip.

**Drafting.** Client updates, replies to the team, write-ups after a call. AI drafts; I read every word and change what doesn't sound like me. Nothing goes out without my yes.

**First-pass reviews.** Before I look at a pull request, AI has already checked it, not just whether the code runs but whether it does what the task actually asked for. It reviews against the acceptance criteria. That one change has worked better than I expected, because the most common problem isn't broken code; it's code that solves a slightly different problem.

**The admin nobody misses.** Turning a call into tasks. Logging time against the right work. Checking a release list against what's actually been approved.

## Where it doesn't

AI is confident. That's the risk.

Early on it told me a set of tasks had been closed. The system had said yes. They hadn't closed; the status hadn't actually changed. Since then the rule is simple: **if it matters, read it back.** A "success" message is not proof something worked.

It also doesn't do the parts of the job that are about people. The difficult conversation, the judgement call on a client, knowing when a developer needs support rather than a deadline. Those stay with me.

## It gets better because I make it

This is the part most people miss. AI doesn't learn from your mistakes on its own. Close the session and the lesson goes with it.

I treat it the way I'd teach a junior developer I want to grow into a principal. **There are steps, and each one has to be taught.** You don't hand a junior the keys to production on day one. You show them how you work, you review what they do, you explain why something was wrong, and you give them more responsibility as they earn it.

So I run retros with it, the same way I would with the team. When something goes wrong, or I correct it, we look at what happened and write the lesson down: in its notes, in its checklists, in the rules it works to. The handoff notes from each piece of work live on my machine, not in a temporary folder that gets wiped. The next session starts from what the last one learned.

**The goal is simple: the same mistake should never happen twice.** Every review, every retro and every correction makes the next week a little better than the last. It compounds.

That's not a feature you switch on. It's a habit, and it's the same habit that makes a good team better over time.

## The rule I work by

Everything AI produces is a draft until a human says yes. For me that's every message, every update and every review. For the team, it's every line of code that gets merged.

It sounds slower. It isn't. The time AI saves on the first draft is far bigger than the time it takes to check it, and the check is what makes it trustworthy.

## What's next

The bigger shift isn't my week; it's the team's. Not everyone uses AI the same way yet, and the best ideas so far have come from developers showing each other what works. That's where I'm putting my effort now.
